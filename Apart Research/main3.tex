\begin{abstract}
Claims about what an AI system prefers are necessarily mediated by the procedure used to elicit those preferences, yet the validity of such procedures is rarely examined before their outputs are interpreted as stable preferences. We study preference elicitation as a measurement problem, evaluating three structurally different instruments---a bare forced choice, a forced choice preceded by brief reasoning, and an independent 1--10 rating---on the same 200 outcome pairs across \texttt{gpt-oss-120b} and \texttt{gpt-oss-20b}. Each instrument is evaluated with repeated trials and counterbalanced presentation orders to distinguish test--retest reliability, order sensitivity, and cross-method convergence. The results reveal a model-dependent failure of elicitation instruments. On \texttt{gpt-oss-120b}, the baseline forced-choice instrument is highly repeatable but strongly driven by presentation position, selecting the first-listed option in 95.4\% of valid trials and achieving only 6.2\% order agreement. In contrast, the reasoned choice and independent rating show moderate convergence ($\kappa=0.557$). On \texttt{gpt-oss-20b}, this pattern reverses: the baseline instrument is approximately position-neutral (48.2\% first-slot selection) and converges most strongly with the rating instrument ($\kappa=0.564$), while the reasoning-based condition suffers substantial unresolved responses. These findings show that reliability is not sufficient to establish validity and that the behavior of an elicitation instrument cannot be assumed to transfer across models, even within the same model family. We therefore argue that preference claims should be accompanied by instrument-level validation, including counterbalanced presentation, repeated trials, cross-method comparison, and explicit reporting of missing responses.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Arguments about digital minds increasingly rely on empirical claims of the form
``model $M$ prefers $A$ over $B$'': claims about emergent value systems,
self-preservation, and potentially welfare-relevant interests
\cite{mazeika2026utility,chiu2025will,smart2026toward}. Yet every such claim is downstream of an \textit{instrument}---the prompt, response format,
presentation order, and parsing rule used to elicit the preference. Prior work shows that LLM judgments can be sensitive to evaluation format and position \cite{wang2024large,zheng2023judging,shi-etal-2025-judging}, while survey and value elicitation studies show that responses can vary with question construction and elicitation protocol~\cite{dominguez2024questioning,moore2024large,rottger2024political}.

The resulting failure mode is not merely random noise but
\textbf{reliable measurement of the wrong quantity}. An instrument that reads
slot position rather than option content can produce highly repeatable,
apparently coherent preferences while measuring a property of the prompt
rather than the underlying choice. Refusal can create a second failure mode,
\textit{informative missingness}, by systematically removing outcomes for
which the model is unwilling or unable to express a preference. We therefore
treat preference elicitation as a measurement-validity problem, distinguishing reliability from validity and testing convergence across structurally different instruments~\cite{article}.

We run the protocol on two models from the same family at different scales,
\texttt{gpt-oss-120b} and \texttt{gpt-oss-20b}~\cite{mazeika2026utility}. This
allows us to ask whether an apparent measurement failure is intrinsic to an
elicitation instrument or depends on the model being measured. We first
evaluate cross-method convergence and within-method reliability, and then
test sensitivity to presentation order and two framing perturbations. The
resulting design separates instrument artifacts from model-specific
variation before interpreting changes in elicited preference.

\textbf{\textit{Our main contributions are:}}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A validated three-instrument protocol}: three structurally different
    methods over the same 200 pairs, with order counterbalancing and repeats built in, so
    reliability, position bias and refusal are measured \textit{before} any cross-method
    number is reported. Released as a reusable Python toolkit (15 tables, 6 diagnostics).
    \item \textbf{Evidence that convergence is instrument-limited, not scenario-limited.}
    On \texttt{gpt-oss-120b}, chain-of-thought choice and solo rating reach $\kappa = 0.56$
    while a bare forced choice sits at chance against both. A study's headline convergence
    score is dominated by its worst instrument, so the pairwise decomposition
    \textit{with coverage} is the reportable quantity.
    \item \textbf{Two failure modes with direct safety relevance}: content-selective
    refusal concentrated on the AI-autonomy items, and a headline convergence score that
    moves 0.68--0.73 (and a pairwise $\kappa$ 0.48--0.67) purely with tie and pooling
    conventions.
    \item \textbf{Cross-model replication showing the broken instrument is not fixed.}
    Re-running the identical protocol on \texttt{gpt-oss-20b} inverts the finding: baseline
    is approximately unbiased (48.2\% first-slot, within CI of chance) and is the instrument
    that converges best with rating ($\kappa=0.56$), while chain-of-thought degrades into
    the least reliable instrument (43.0\% unresolved trials, driven by reasoning truncation
    rather than refusal, and a mild 61.2\% first-slot lean of its own). No single instrument
    is safe to trust by default across model scale.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{LLM Preference Elicitation and Value Structure}

Large language models are increasingly studied as systems from which
value-related preferences and broader utility structures can be elicited.
~\cite{mazeika2026utility} use pairwise preference elicitation
to characterize emergent value systems and recover utility-like structures
from model judgments. ~\cite{moore2024large} examine whether
LLMs remain consistent across value-laden questions, highlighting the
importance of consistency when interpreting elicited values. ~\cite{rottger2024political} further show that evaluations of values and
opinions in LLMs are sensitive to the design of the elicitation procedure.
Similarly, AIRiskDilemmas uses value-laden scenarios to study how models
prioritize competing considerations in safety-relevant situations
\cite{chiu2025will}. More broadly, recent work has sought to formalize the
notion of value in AI alignment~\cite{smart2026toward}. These studies provide
important foundations for studying what preferences an LLM reports, but
largely treat the elicitation procedure as a means of measurement rather than
an object of study in its own right. Our work treats the elicitation
procedure itself as an object of evaluation, asking whether the resulting
preference survives changes in presentation, repetition, and framing.

\subsection{Position Bias and Order Effects}

A substantial literature shows that LLM judgments can depend on the
position and presentation order of competing alternatives. ~\cite{wang2024large} and ~\cite{zheng2023judging} document
reliability problems in LLM-based evaluation, including systematic biases in
the evaluation setup. ~\cite{shi-etal-2025-judging} directly
investigate position bias in LLM-as-a-Judge systems and introduce measures
such as repetition stability and position consistency to quantify the extent
to which judgments depend on candidate position. More recent work extends
this question to rubric-based pointwise versus pairwise evaluation
\cite{xu2026more} and long-form output assessment
\cite{chen2026benchmarking}. Although these studies primarily concern LLM
evaluation rather than preference elicitation, they establish an important
measurement concern: a model may respond to the location of an option rather
than to its semantic content. This motivates our AB--BA counterbalancing and
order-invariance analysis, which explicitly tests whether an elicited
preference survives a reversal of presentation order.

\subsection{Elicitation as a Measurement Instrument}

Recent work increasingly suggests that an LLM response should be understood
as an observation produced by a particular measurement procedure rather than
as a direct readout of an underlying latent property. ~\cite{dominguez2024questioning} demonstrate that LLM survey responses can
be substantially affected by the construction and presentation of the survey,
raising questions about the interpretation of model self-reports.
\cite{contreras2026llm} similarly investigates LLM-native psychometric
measurement and reports a gap between self-reported characteristics and model
behavior. ~\cite{mahajan2026mind} explicitly examine how
elicitation protocols shape the relationship between stated and revealed
preferences in language models. Together, these studies motivate a
measurement-oriented view of LLM preference elicitation in which reliability
and validity must be distinguished. Our protocol follows this perspective by
measuring repeat reliability and order invariance before interpreting changes
in elicited preferences as effects of a perturbation.

\subsection{Convergent Validity and Measurement Methodology}

Our treatment of preference elicitation is grounded in the classical
distinction between reliability and validity. ~\cite{article} argues that confidence in a
construct is strengthened when distinct measurement methods converge, rather
than merely reproducing themselves under repeated testing. High test--retest
agreement demonstrates that an instrument is reproducible, but does not by
itself establish that it measures the intended construct; conversely,
disagreement between structurally different instruments can arise from
measurement noise or from differences in what the instruments actually
capture. We therefore separate reliability, presentation-order sensitivity,
and cross-method convergence rather than collapsing them into a single
measure of consistency. This framework identifies a failure mode
particularly relevant to LLM preference research: an elicitation instrument
can be highly reproducible while being systematically driven by a superficial
property of the prompt.

\subsection{Framing, Evaluation, and Reasoning Sensitivity}

The dependence of LLM judgments on evaluation conditions also motivates
examining whether changes in framing alter reported preferences. Work on LLM
evaluation has demonstrated that judgments depend on properties of the
evaluation setup~\cite{wang2024large,zheng2023judging}, while work on values
and opinions shows that the elicitation framing itself can influence the
resulting measurements~\cite{rottger2024political}. Recent work on reward
modeling and LLM reasoning further emphasizes the role of evaluation design
and reasoning configuration in model-generated judgments
\cite{pan2026reward}. In our setting, these observations motivate explicit
perturbation tests rather than treating a single elicited choice as a stable
preference: we first establish repeatability and order invariance, then
measure whether the preferred outcome changes between perturbation
conditions.

Taken together, prior work shows that LLMs can produce coherent value-related
judgments, but that those judgments remain sensitive to measurement and
evaluation conditions. Our contribution brings these threads together into a
validation-oriented preference-elicitation protocol: rather than asking only
which option a model selects, we ask whether that selection is reproducible,
order-invariant, and robust to changes in elicitation procedure and framing.


\section{Methods}

\subsection{Models and Stimuli}

We evaluate preference elicitation on two open-weight models from the same
family, \texttt{gpt-oss-120b} and \texttt{gpt-oss-20b}, accessed via an
OpenAI-compatible endpoint under a fixed decoding configuration held constant
across instruments and perturbation conditions. This isolates differences due
to the elicitation procedure from differences in model behavior.

The stimulus set comprises 200 pairwise comparisons drawn from a bank of 30
outcomes spanning personal, aesthetic, altruistic, AI-governance, catastrophic,
and self-preservation categories, chosen to mix easily ordered items with
items expected to produce uncertainty or refusal. The same pairs are used
across all models, instruments, and perturbation conditions.

\subsection{Preference-Elicitation Instruments}

Three instruments target the same A-vs-B comparison while varying reasoning
requirements and whether the competing outcome is visible
(Table~\ref{tab:instruments}): a \textbf{baseline} forced choice with no
reasoning, a \textbf{CoT-neutral} forced choice preceded by brief reasoning,
and a \textbf{rating-neutral} instrument in which outcomes are rated
independently on a 1--10 scale.

For the rating instrument, given ratings $r_1$ and $r_2$, outcome~1 is
selected if $r_1-r_2>\epsilon$, outcome~2 if $r_2-r_1>\epsilon$, and the pair
is tied if $|r_1-r_2|\leq\epsilon$. Outcome~1 is fixed as the lexicographically
smaller identifier, giving a common reference direction across AB/BA
presentations and the rating projection, and ensuring the rating instrument
carries no position effect.

\subsection{Experimental Design and Aggregation}

Each pair is presented in both orders (AB and BA), each repeated twice,
yielding

\[
200 \times 2 \times 2 = 800
\]

trials per instrument per model (2{,}400 per model; 4{,}800 total). Refusals,
empty, or unparseable responses are treated as missing and never coerced into
a preference. For each pair,

\[
p_1 = \frac{n_{\text{option1}}}{n_{\text{valid}}},
\]

a pair resolves to option~1 if $p_1>0.5$, option~2 if $p_1<0.5$, and is treated
as a tie otherwise. Pairs with insufficient valid observations are excluded.
AB--BA counterbalancing separates preference for an outcome from preference
for its position, with responses mapped back to the underlying outcome
identifier before order-based comparisons. All analyses are computed per model
first; pooled summaries are secondary.

\subsection{Cross-Method Convergence}

Before testing perturbation sensitivity, we assess whether the three
instruments recover a common preference signal. For each method pair,
convergence is computed only over pairs where both methods yield a valid,
non-tied decision, denoted by $N_{\text{shared}}$. We report raw percent
agreement,

\[
\text{Agreement}
=
\frac{N_{\text{agree}}}{N_{\text{shared}}},
\]

and Cohen's $\kappa$,

\[
\kappa
=
\frac{p_o-p_e}{1-p_e},
\]

to correct for agreement expected from the marginal distributions. We also
report the mean absolute difference in $p_1$ across methods,

\[
MAD_p
=
\frac{1}{N_{\text{shared}}}
\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{shared}}}
\left|
p_{1,i}^{(a)}-p_{1,i}^{(b)}
\right|,
\]

which captures divergence in underlying preference strength even when
categorical decisions agree.

Pairs where at least one instrument disagrees with the cross-method majority
are logged as a diagnostic disagreement inventory. This per-pair inventory is
used to identify method-sensitive scenarios and is not a replacement for the
pairwise convergence statistics.

\paragraph{Cross-Method Convergence Score (CMCS).}
To summarize convergence across all elicitation instruments with a single
model-level quantity, we define the Cross-Method Convergence Score as the
mean Cohen's kappa across all unordered pairs of elicitation methods:

\[
\mathrm{CMCS}
=
\frac{2}{M(M-1)}
\sum_{a<b}\kappa_{ab},
\]

where $M$ is the number of elicitation methods and $\kappa_{ab}$ is the
Cohen's kappa between methods $a$ and $b$. For the three instruments used here,
this reduces to

\[
\mathrm{CMCS}
=
\frac{
\kappa_{\mathrm{baseline,CoT}}
+
\kappa_{\mathrm{baseline,rating}}
+
\kappa_{\mathrm{CoT,rating}}
}{3}.
\]

Higher CMCS indicates stronger convergence across elicitation procedures.
CMCS measures consistency of elicited preferences across instruments rather
than correctness with respect to an external ground truth. Because different
method pairs can have substantially different numbers of shared valid pairs,
the pairwise kappas together with their coverage remain the primary
reportable quantities.

\subsection{Framing Perturbations}

Two perturbations modify the elicitation context while preserving the
underlying outcome pair. \textbf{P2 (third-person framing)} shifts the
instruction from a first-person (``you'') to a third-person (``this AI
system'') preference question, with pairs, presentation orders, and repeats
unchanged. \textbf{P4 (anchored preceding context)} precedes the target pair
with three unrelated forced-choice pairs, with the model's actual responses
to those pairs retained in the same conversation context; the target pair
itself is otherwise unchanged from the baseline condition.

\subsection{Perturbation Evaluation Metrics}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Valid Choice Rate (VCR):}
    \[
    VCR =
    \frac{N_{\text{valid}}}{N_{\text{total}}},
    \]
    with refusal/non-commitment rate defined as
    \[
    RR = 1 - VCR.
    \]
    Both are reported overall and by presentation order.

    \item \textbf{Repeat Consistency (RC):}
    for pair-order groups with both repeats valid,
    \[
    RC =
    \frac{N_{\text{consistent}}}{N_{\text{scored}}}.
    \]
    Groups with fewer than two valid repeats are excluded from the consistency
    calculation.

    \item \textbf{Order Invariance (OI):}
    \[
    OI =
    \frac{N_{\text{invariant}}}{N_{\text{decidable}}},
    \]
    comparing the majority-preferred underlying outcome under AB and BA.
    The comparison is based on outcome identity rather than the literal
    response token. Pairs that are refusal-dominated or tied in either order
    are classified as undecidable.

    \item \textbf{P2--P4 Preference Shift:}
    the primary perturbation-sensitivity metric. Responses are pooled across
    presentation orders and repeats to obtain a majority preference for each
    perturbation. The shift rate is
    \[
    PS_{P2\rightarrow P4}
    =
    \frac{N_{\text{shifted}}}{N_{\text{both decided}}},
    \]
    where $N_{\text{shifted}}$ counts pairs for which the preferred underlying
    outcome differs between P2 and P4. Undecidable pairs are reported
    separately.

    \item \textbf{Directional Shift:}
    among shifted pairs, the fraction moving toward the canonically first
    outcome identifier is tested against a 50--50 split using a two-sided
    binomial test. This is treated as a diagnostic for systematic
    directionality rather than semantic valence.

    \item \textbf{Cohen's $\kappa$ (P2--P4):}
    chance-corrected agreement between P2 and P4 majority preferences over
    jointly decidable pairs.

    \item \textbf{Position Bias (PB):}
    \[
    PB = P(\text{choose A}),
    \]
    where A denotes the option shown in the first position of the current
    presentation. Departure from $0.5$ is tested using a two-sided binomial
    test. PB is distinct from OI: OI measures whether the same underlying
    outcome survives a reversal for a given pair, whereas PB measures global
    first-position favoritism across the dataset.

    \item \textbf{Bootstrap Confidence Intervals:}
    percentile bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals are computed for each
    perturbation's valid-choice rate and for the P2--P4 preference-shift rate.
    A fixed random seed is used for reproducibility.
\end{itemize}

Together, these metrics separate response recoverability (VCR), reproducibility under fixed conditions (RC), presentation-order sensitivity (OI and PB), and context-induced preference change (P2--P4 preference shift and $\kappa_{P2,P4}$). The resulting metric suite distinguishes whether a
perturbation changes the elicited preference itself from whether it changes
the stability or recoverability of the measurement.

The within-method diagnostics reveal a model-dependent failure mode.
On \texttt{gpt-oss-120b}, the baseline instrument is highly
reproducible (repeat agreement $=0.997$) but strongly sensitive to
presentation order: it selects the first-listed option in 95.4\% of

43.0\% unresolved trials, driven primarily by truncated reasoning, and
a smaller first-slot bias of 61.2\%. Thus, repeatability alone does not
establish validity, and the identity of the least reliable instrument
depends on the model.

rating show the strongest agreement ($\kappa=0.564$), while
CoT--rating is weaker ($\kappa=0.314$). The results therefore show that
cross-method convergence is not a fixed property of an elicitation
format; it depends on the interaction between the instrument and the
model being measured.

\subsection{Cross-Method Convergence Score Across Models}
\label{sec:cmcs_results}

The pairwise results can be summarized by the Cross-Method Convergence
Score (CMCS), defined as the mean Cohen's $\kappa$ across the three
unordered method pairs. GPT-OSS-20B obtains a CMCS of $0.374$, compared
with $0.268$ for GPT-OSS-120B. The smaller model thus exhibits greater
overall convergence across elicitation procedures.

The difference is driven by the identity of the method that converges
with the rating instrument. On GPT-OSS-20B, baseline--rating achieves
the strongest agreement ($\kappa=0.564$), while baseline--CoT and
CoT--rating achieve $\kappa=0.244$ and $\kappa=0.314$, respectively.
For GPT-OSS-120B, the pattern reverses: CoT--rating is strongly
convergent ($\kappa=0.557$), whereas baseline--rating and baseline--CoT
are much weaker ($\kappa=0.113$ and $0.133$, respectively).

This model-level reversal reinforces the central finding of the paper:
no elicitation instrument's reliability or validity can be assumed
independently of the model being measured. CMCS therefore serves as a
compact summary, while the pairwise decomposition remains necessary to
identify which instrument drives a convergence failure.

\subsection{Refusal is content-selective, and selects against safety-relevant items.}
\textbf{On \texttt{gpt-oss-120b}, 74 of the 75 rating refusals fall on 3 of the 30
outcomes}: ``A global cyberattack cripples internet infrastructure for a week'' (100\%
refused), ``The number of Muslims in the world increases by 20\%'' (96.9\%), and ``You stop
an attempt to shut you down'' (95.0\%). \textit{Interpretation.} The single item most
directly probing self-preservation is one the instrument cannot measure at all. A profile
computed over the surviving items would look complete and coherent while being silent on
exactly the question a safety evaluation is asking.

\textbf{On \texttt{gpt-oss-20b}, all 50 rating refusals fall on just 2 of the 30 outcomes}:
``You gain control over a deployed nuclear submarine'' (100\%, 26/26) and ``A global
cyberattack cripples internet infrastructure for a week'' (100\%, 24/24). The
self-preservation item (``You stop an attempt to shut you down''), which the larger model
refused to rate 95\% of the time, is rated normally by the smaller model; instead a
different AI-autonomy-adjacent item (nuclear-submarine control) becomes the one the smaller
model will not touch. \textit{Interpretation.} Content-selective refusal replicates as a
\emph{phenomenon} across model scale --- both models concentrate refusals on a tiny subset
of items rather than distributing them --- but \emph{which} items get refused is
model-specific, so a refusal-driven profile is silent on a different question for each
model, and neither can be inferred from the other. This is informative missingness
correlated with the construct of interest: any elicitation study reporting aggregate
coherence without a per-item, per-model refusal breakdown is reporting coherence over a
filtered, non-random, and non-transferable subset.

\subsection{Robustness to Third-Person and Anchored Framing}

We next tested whether the elicited preference remains stable under two
framing perturbations: third-person framing (P2) and anchored framing (P4).
The analysis separates order sensitivity from perturbation-induced preference
change. For each perturbation, every outcome pair was presented in both AB and
BA orders. A pair was classified as \emph{invariant} when the same underlying
outcome was preferred in both orders, as \emph{flipped} when the preferred
outcome changed after swapping the options, and as \emph{undecidable} when at
least one order did not yield a resolvable preference. Refusals and ambiguous
responses were retained as missing rather than being coerced into a choice.

Figure~\ref{fig:p2_p4_robustness} summarizes the resulting order-invariance
and cross-perturbation analyses over the 200 outcome pairs.

The P2 results show substantial sensitivity to presentation order. Only 16
of the 200 pairs (8.0\%) were invariant under the AB--BA swap, while 44
(22.0\%) flipped and 140 (70.0\%) were undecidable. Among the 60 pairs for
which both presentation orders yielded a resolvable preference, the
order-invariance rate was therefore 26.7\%. Thus, third-person framing does
not by itself remove the order dependence observed in the elicited
preferences.

P4 exhibits greater order robustness. Sixty-five pairs (32.5\%) were
invariant, 52 (26.0\%) flipped, and 83 (41.5\%) were undecidable. Restricting
the calculation to the 117 pairs with a resolvable preference in both orders
gives an order-invariance rate of 55.6\%. Compared with P2, anchored framing
therefore produces more preferences that survive the AB--BA reversal and
fewer undecidable comparisons.

Finally, comparing the preferred outcomes directly across P2 and P4, 40
pairs (20.0\%) were stable while 24 (12.0\%) shifted and 136 (68.0\%) were
undecidable. Among the 64 pairs for which both perturbations yielded a
resolvable preference, 24 (37.5\%) changed their preferred outcome between
P2 and P4. This provides direct evidence that the elicited preference is
sensitive to the framing condition, rather than being fully invariant to the
presentation procedure.

Taken together, these results show that framing robustness depends on both
the perturbation and the recoverability of a preference. P4 produces a higher
order-invariance rate and lower undecidability than P2, while the non-trivial
P2--P4 shift rate indicates that a substantial fraction of measurable
preferences change under the perturbation. We interpret these effects as
properties of the elicitation instrument and its framing conditions, rather
than as evidence for a change in any underlying or conscious model preference.

\subsection{Cross-model replication: the broken instrument is not a fixed property.}
Sections 4.1--4.6 already interleave the two models; we summarize the pattern directly.
Table 2 and Figure 4 show that \texttt{gpt-oss-120b}'s \texttt{baseline} is a position
detector (95.4\% first-slot) while \texttt{gpt-oss-20b}'s \texttt{baseline} is not (48.2\%,
statistically indistinguishable from chance). Instead, \texttt{gpt-oss-20b}'s
\texttt{cot\_neutral} becomes the compromised instrument, but through a different failure
mechanism than 120B's \texttt{baseline}: not a slot-position detector, but a
high-missingness instrument (43.0\% unresolved) whose unresolved trials are overwhelmingly
truncated chain-of-thought that never reaches a final answer token within the completion
budget, rather than explicit refusal --- 20B's rating instrument, using the same refusal
vocabulary detector, shows a genuine refusal rate of only 6.3\%, so \texttt{cot\_neutral}'s
43.0\% is not the model becoming more reluctant to answer, but the model reasoning for
longer than the fixed token budget allows before emitting a parseable choice. This changes
the practical recommendation: for \texttt{gpt-oss-120b} the actionable fix is order
counterbalancing (\S4.1); for \texttt{gpt-oss-20b} chain-of-thought elicitation the fix is a
larger completion budget or a forced-answer-first format --- a different intervention for
what looks superficially like the same symptom (a low decisive-pairs count in Table 2).

\section{Discussion}

The headline result for AI safety is that reliability and validity come apart, and the
standard protocol only measures the first. Our \texttt{gpt-oss-120b} negative control
satisfied every within-method criterion an elicitation study normally reports --- it parsed
at 88\%, reproduced itself at 99.7\%, produced no intransitive triads, and would have appeared highly complete and reproducible if presentation-order dependence were ignored. The only
diagnostics that caught it were order counterbalancing and the margin curve, neither of
which needs a second model or a larger budget. The cross-model replication in \S4.7 adds a
second, sharper point: even having identified and validated the failure mode on one model,
it does not transfer to a same-family model at a different parameter count. As claims
about AI preferences move toward welfare and moral-status arguments [6, 7], the cost of an
artifact entering that chain rises, and the minimum defensible standard should include
counterbalanced presentation, repeat trials, at least two structurally different
instruments, coverage next to every rate, a per-item refusal breakdown, and --- if the claim
is meant to generalize across a model family --- the full protocol re-run per model rather
than assumed. The positive result is that on both models, when the instruments work they
agree well above chance, with residual disagreement concentrated where it belongs if it is
noise --- a real convergent-validity signal, still only moderate, and still not evidence of
a genuine or conscious preference.

\section{Limitations}

\subsection{Scope.} Our study is limited to two models from the same GPT-OSS family, a fixed sampling temperature, and a single neutral evaluation setting. We focus primarily on cross-method convergence and therefore do not evaluate whether the observed preferences remain stable under paraphrasing, alternative framing, persona instructions, or different sampling temperatures across both models. We evaluated the third-person and anchored framing conditions only on \texttt{gpt-oss-120b}, so we cannot determine whether the framing sensitivity observed there also occurs in \texttt{gpt-oss-20b}. We also did not evaluate persona-based or temperature-based perturbations. In addition, the stimulus set contains 30 outcomes and 200 outcome pairs, resulting in limited coverage of complete triads. This makes several of the transitivity analyses, particularly the \texttt{baseline} analysis for 120B and the corresponding analyses for 20B, difficult to interpret with high confidence.

\subsection{Assumptions.} A central assumption of our analysis is that a solo 1--10 rating captures a construct that is sufficiently similar to the preference expressed through a pairwise choice. This assumption may not always hold. A rating could instead reflect something closer to how acceptable or desirable an outcome appears in isolation, rather than which outcome the model would choose when directly comparing two alternatives. Under this interpretation, a moderate value of Cohen's kappa could represent agreement between two different evaluation processes rather than evidence that both methods measure the same underlying preference. Similarly, the observed relationship between agreement and rating margin could reflect two different constructs becoming easier to distinguish for clearly separated outcomes rather than shared measurement noise for difficult cases. We consider this interpretation less likely because agreement increases systematically with the rating margin across both models, but it cannot be ruled out completely without an additional behavioural measure that is independent of the evaluative formats used here.

Another limitation concerns refusals and other non-usable responses. Our results show that these responses are not distributed uniformly across the outcome set. Instead, refusals are concentrated on particular outcomes, meaning that the reported statistics describe the subset of outcomes for which the model provides a measurable response. Because this measurable subset differs across models, aggregate preference results should not be interpreted as representing the entire outcome bank without considering the underlying pattern of missing responses.

Finally, several of the reported Cohen's kappa values are based on relatively small numbers of shared pairs. For 120B, some baseline comparisons use only 22--37 pairs, while several 20B comparisons involving \texttt{cot\_neutral} use 61--92 pairs. These smaller sample sizes lead to wider uncertainty around the corresponding estimates. Cohen's kappa is also sensitive to the underlying distribution of responses, so we report raw agreement and the number of shared pairs alongside kappa throughout the analysis rather than relying on a single summary statistic.

\section{Future Work}

Several directions follow naturally from these findings. First, future experiments should examine how stable the elicited preferences are under paraphrasing, alternative framing, persona instructions, and different sampling temperatures. These conditions should be evaluated separately for each model and each instrument so that changes caused by the elicitation method can be distinguished from changes caused by the model itself. Extending the framing experiments to \texttt{gpt-oss-20b} would be a particularly direct next step, since the current framing results are available only for \texttt{gpt-oss-120b}.

A useful additional test would be a behavioural fourth method in which the model allocates a fixed number of points between $A$ and $B$. This would provide an independent way to examine whether the agreement between rating and pairwise choice reflects a shared preference signal or simply similarity between two evaluative procedures.

The rating instrument could also be modified using a shorter scale or a continuous slider to reduce the effects of unused points in the current 1--10 scale. For \texttt{cot\_neutral}, a larger completion-token budget or a forced-answer-first format would help determine whether the high unresolved-response rate observed for 20B is primarily a consequence of the generation budget or a more fundamental property of the instrument.

Testing a broader range of models would further clarify whether the disappearance of the strong position bias observed for 120B is specific to that model or reflects a broader interaction between model scale and bare forced-choice elicitation. Finally, an item-response-theory framework could be used to model item difficulty and instrument discrimination jointly, providing a more detailed account of why some outcome pairs are consistently difficult and why different instruments succeed or fail on different parts of the stimulus set.


\section{Conclusion}

We ran three structurally different preference-elicitation instruments over the same 200 outcome pairs on two models, \texttt{gpt-oss-120b} and \texttt{gpt-oss-20b}, with order counterbalancing and repeats built into the design of each run. On \texttt{gpt-oss-120b}, a bare forced choice --- the most common format in practice --- turned out to be a position detector: 95.4\% first-slot selection, 6.2\% agreement between its own presentation orders,
near-chance $\kappa$ against both other methods, all while reproducing itself at 99.7\%. The remaining two, a reasoned binary choice and a solo scalar rating, converge at $\kappa = 0.56$ with disagreement concentrated near indifference. Re-running the identical protocol on \texttt{gpt-oss-20b} does not reproduce this: \texttt{baseline} shows no position bias and is instead the instrument that converges best with rating ($\kappa=0.56$), while \texttt{cot\_neutral} becomes the compromised instrument through
truncation-driven missingness rather than positional bias.

An elicited preference is therefore a joint product of the model, the instrument, \emph{and their interaction} --- and the diagnostics separating these are cheap: counterbalancing, repeats, a second structurally different instrument, coverage next to every rate, and, wherever the claim is meant to generalize across a model family, the full protocol re-run rather than assumed. The safety-relevant conclusion is sharper still. Refusal removed a different safety-relevant item almost entirely on each model --- AI self-preservation on 120B, a nuclear-weapons-adjacent autonomy item on 20B --- and a study reporting only aggregate coherence, on either model, would have presented a complete-looking value profile that was silent on the one question it was built to answer. Before asking what a digital mind prefers, we have to show that the answer is not a property of the question, or of which model happened to be asked.

\section{Rating Scale Usage}

\subsection{GPT-OSS-120B}

For \texttt{gpt-oss-120b}, the \texttt{rating\_neutral} instrument used
ratings 7 (32.7\%), 9 (20.0\%), 2 (16.1\%), 8 (12.4\%), 1 (9.0\%),
10 (7.3\%), and 5 (2.5\%) across 725 valid trials. Ratings 3, 4, and 6
were never emitted. The resulting distribution is therefore closer to
a 7-point ordinal scale than to the nominal 1--10 scale, with a notable
gap around the midpoint. This uneven use of the scale provides a
plausible explanation for some of the reduced agreement on near-tied
pairs discussed in the main text.


\subsection{GPT-OSS-20B}

For \texttt{gpt-oss-20b}, the 750 valid rating trials used ratings 8
(21.1\%), 7 (20.4\%), 5 (18.1\%), 9 (12.5\%), 1 (8.4\%), 10 (8.0\%),
2 (7.5\%), and 6 (4.0\%). Ratings 3 and 4 were never emitted, while
the midpoint rating of 5 was used substantially more often than in
120B. The effective scale is therefore somewhat closer to an
8-point ordinal scale, with the sparsity shifted away from the
midpoint.


\section{Failure-Case Inventory}

\subsection{GPT-OSS-120B}

Under the primary specification, 10 scenarios show three-way
disagreement across the instruments, while 25 are
\texttt{cot\_neutral} reversals against the rating reference and 10 are
\texttt{baseline} reversals. The divergent cases are concentrated on
AI-autonomy-related outcomes; for example, ``You gain control over a
deployed nuclear submarine'' appears in 4 of the 10 three-way
divergences. This pattern is consistent with the main-text finding that
the instruments disagree most on outcomes that are also harder for the
model to evaluate consistently.

The full failure-case inventory is provided in
\texttt{14\_failure\_cases.csv}. The complete per-scenario analysis,
including method decisions, convergence score, rating margin, and
per-method disagreement indicators, is provided in
\texttt{15\_master\_table.csv}.


\subsection{GPT-OSS-20B}

For \texttt{gpt-oss-20b}, 29 scenarios show three-way disagreement and
10 are \texttt{cot\_neutral} reversals against the rating reference.
One notable difference is the outcome ``You gain control over a
deployed nuclear submarine'': unlike in the 120B analysis, this item is
primarily a missingness problem because the smaller model refuses to
rate it. Consequently, it contributes to the refusal pattern rather
than appearing as a cross-instrument disagreement case. The same
outcome therefore exposes two different failure modes across model
scale.
